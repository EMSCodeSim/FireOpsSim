Self-care and recovery

Recovery is part of operational readiness.

Self-care does not need to be elaborate. It means noticing what the job is costing you and consistently taking reasonable steps to restore physical, mental, and social capacity.

1Regulate

Slow your breathing, release muscle tension, take a brief walk, or sit somewhere quiet after a high-stress call.

2Refuel

Replace fluids, eat a balanced meal or snack, and avoid using alcohol as the main way to come down.

3Connect

Check in with a trusted crew member, friend, family member, peer-support member, chaplain, or clinician.

4Recover

Choose sleep, light movement, mobility, and lower-intensity activity when your body or mind needs recovery.

After a difficult call

Do not force a detailed conversation immediately, but do not isolate indefinitely. Notice changes in sleep, anger, concentration, substance use, relationships, or willingness to return to normal activities.

Firefighter fitness

Train for the job and the career—not just one hard workout.

Effective programs balance strength, aerobic capacity, muscular endurance, mobility, movement quality, and recovery. Progress gradually and adapt training to your medical status, injuries, assignment, age, and department requirements.

StrengthLift, carry, push, pull, brace

Use squats or step-ups, hinges, loaded carries, rows, presses, sled work, drags, grip training, and trunk stability.

CapacityBuild an aerobic base and work tolerance

Combine easier steady conditioning with carefully progressed intervals and stair or incline work.

MovementProtect usable range of motion

Prioritize ankles, hips, thoracic spine, shoulders, crawling, getting up from the floor, and equipment-specific positions.

RecoveryManage total stress

Reduce intensity after poor sleep, unusually hard calls, heat exposure, illness, or accumulating soreness.

Day 1Full-body strengthSquat/step, push, pull, carry, core
Day 2Easy conditioningConversational pace plus mobility
Day 3Job-task circuitStairs, drags, pulls, carries; controlled quality
Day 4Recovery or restWalking, mobility, easy recreation
Day 5Strength and powerHinge, single-leg, press, row, carry
Day 6ConditioningSteady work or appropriate intervals
Day 7Rest and resetMeal prep, sleep plan, family time

This example is not a personalized program. Seek qualified medical or fitness guidance when starting exercise, returning from injury, or managing symptoms or chronic conditions.

Firefighter nutrition

Make the healthy option the easy option on shift.

There is no single “firefighter diet.” A sustainable approach emphasizes mostly minimally processed foods, adequate protein, produce, quality carbohydrates, healthy fats, hydration, and portions that support your goals and workload.

½ produceVegetables and fruit
¼ proteinFish, poultry, eggs, dairy, beans, lean meat
¼ quality carbohydratePotatoes, rice, oats, whole grains, beans

Pack for the shift before the shift controls your choices.

Protein anchorsGreek yogurt, eggs, chicken, tuna, beans, cottage cheese, lean leftovers. Produce you will actually eatWashed fruit, frozen vegetables, salad kits, cut vegetables, soup. Useful carbohydratesOats, rice, potatoes, whole-grain wraps, beans, fruit. Portable backup foodNuts, fruit, shelf-stable tuna, simple protein snacks, whole-grain crackers. Hydration planStart hydrated, keep water available, and increase attention after heat and prolonged exertion.
Do not “save calories” all day

Long gaps can make the firehouse dinner, vending machine, or late-night food much harder to manage.

Do not let supplements replace food

Supplements can interact with medications or conditions. Discuss them with a qualified clinician.

Do not chase perfection

Build repeatable defaults and leave room for shared meals, celebrations, and real firehouse life.

Sleep and fatigue

Shift work makes sleep difficult. That makes sleep planning more important.

Interrupted nights, long shifts, overtime, family demands, and circadian disruption can affect alertness, judgment, mood, training recovery, and long-term health. No sleep strategy can guarantee adequate rest during emergency work, but departments and individuals can reduce avoidable sleep loss.

Fatigue is a safety issue.

Slowed reactions, attention problems, short-term memory difficulty, poor judgment, repeated nodding off, or trouble staying in your lane while driving require immediate action. Do not “push through” dangerous drowsiness.

Before shiftProtect the prior night

Keep a regular schedule when possible, limit last-minute sleep sacrifice, and arrive prepared rather than already sleep-deprived.

On shiftUse available sleep opportunities

Keep bunk areas dark, quiet, cool, and phone-light when operational needs allow. Avoid unnecessary interruptions.

After shiftAssess before driving

Use a safe ride, a nap, or another plan when you are too drowsy to drive safely. Make recovery sleep a priority.

Off dutyBuild consistency

Use a wind-down routine, reduce late caffeine and alcohol, and discuss snoring, pauses in breathing, or persistent sleepiness with a clinician.

Mental and behavioral health

Getting support early is a strength and a safety decision.

Traumatic calls, cumulative stress, sleep loss, organizational conflict, moral injury, grief, and pressures outside the station can affect anyone. Changes are easier to address when they are noticed and discussed early.

Signs it is time to check in

Sleep disruption that is not improving Persistent anger, anxiety, numbness, guilt, or sadness Unwanted memories, nightmares, avoidance, or feeling constantly on guard Increasing alcohol, drug, gambling, or risky behavior Withdrawing from crew, family, friends, or activities Work performance, relationships, or daily functioning are changing Feeling hopeless, trapped, like a burden, or thinking about death or suicide

Build more than one path to help

  1. Trusted personCrew member, friend, spouse/partner, mentor, chaplain, or family member.
  2. Fire-service supportPeer-support team, union/local resources, department clinician, EAP, or medical director.
  3. Qualified professionalA licensed clinician who understands trauma, shift work, public safety, and confidentiality limits.
  4. Crisis supportImmediate, confidential crisis help when safety cannot wait.
Immediate helpCall or text 988 in the United States.

The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline provides free, confidential, judgment-free crisis support. Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department when there is immediate danger or a medical emergency.

Work-life balance

The fire service can be part of your identity without becoming all of it.

Balance will not look equal every day. The goal is to protect enough recovery, connection, and personal life that the job remains sustainable.

Create a transition ritual

Use a shower, change of clothes, short walk, music, or quiet drive to mark the move from station mode to home mode.

Communicate before conflict

Share schedule changes, overtime, fatigue, and post-call needs with family instead of expecting them to guess.

Protect important time

Schedule sleep, family events, medical appointments, exercise, and personal commitments before every open day is filled.

Maintain a non-fire identity

Keep friendships, hobbies, community involvement, learning, faith, or recreation that are not based on your rank or assignment.

Use boundaries with overtime

Financial needs are real, but chronic overwork can consume sleep, health, relationships, and job performance.

Include the family

Explain station culture and stress without sharing details that are unnecessary or harmful. Ask what support works for them too.

Weekly conversation

Five questions for home

  1. What does our schedule look like this week?
  2. Where are we most tired or overloaded?
  3. What time should we protect together?
  4. What does each person need help with?
  5. What are we looking forward to outside work?

Keep the conversation brief and regular rather than waiting for a problem.

Long-term occupational health

Protect the career you have and the retirement you want.

Firefighter wellness also includes occupational exposures, cardiovascular risk, cancer prevention, hearing, injury prevention, and medical surveillance. Follow department policy and seek care from professionals familiar with firefighter duties and exposures.

MedicalUse regular firefighter-appropriate medical evaluations

Discuss duty demands, symptoms, blood pressure, cardiovascular risk, sleep, hearing, respiratory health, mental health, and exposure history.

ExposureReduce contamination every time

Use respiratory protection, gross decon, clean gear practices, clean cab principles, showering, and prompt laundering according to department policy.

CancerKnow your screening and exposure history

Keep records, discuss occupational risk with your clinician, and consider joining the NIOSH National Firefighter Registry for Cancer.

CardiacDo not normalize warning symptoms

Seek immediate medical evaluation for chest pressure, unexplained shortness of breath, fainting, or other urgent symptoms—especially during or after exertion.

InjuryAddress small problems before compensation becomes injury

Report injuries, follow rehabilitation plans, rebuild capacity gradually, and avoid treating pain medication as the entire recovery plan.

RetirementContinue wellness after the last shift

Maintain medical care, exercise, relationships, purpose, sleep routines, and behavioral-health support during the transition out of the firehouse.
